Overcoming The Mimic Within  
 Posted by  varma   love4vishal@rediffmail.com

A woman is elevated within a business hierarchy and then becomes a managerial monster. Her ego begins to inflate, she becomes distant with former peers, and she appears condescending as well as abrupt with subordinates. This scenario might sound farfetched, but it actually is a scene I observed more than once in my 20-year career in Corporate America.

When I started scrutinizing such situations in more detail, it occurred to me that many of the professional women I watched transform into these management monstrosities essentially were bright, ambitious, and hardworking. However, somewhere along the way, they all had tended to pattern their behaviors after the businessmen with whom they interacted. Instead of drawing upon their unique abilities, they seemed to fall into a routine of what I label "workplace mimicry."

Emulating the managerial styles of male colleagues appears to be a prevailing technique used by many aspiring businesswomen to blend into traditional organizational hierarchies. Assimilation, though, comes at a hefty price. Workplace mimicry tends to overshadow a woman`s inherent qualities, predisposes her to display exaggerated versions of male supervisory behaviors and can fail in fulfilling its oft-intended purpose - career advancement. I have noticed within the business world far too many talented, capable women who sold themselves short by choosing to mimic the managerial idiosyncrasies of the men around them. All signs indicated that these women could have been more productive in their jobs and effective in their professional development if they had simply been themselves, while applying their individual skills and natural demeanors to their responsibilities.

Specific styles of conduct seem to fuel workplace mimicry. For instance, these are the kinds of managerial behaviors I repeatedly observed being imitated by female colleagues during my years in Corporate America.

Mimicry Behavior - Operating on a need-to-know basis . . .

Information is power in the business sector, and some managers take that notion to an extreme. They are the ones who share information with employees only when necessary, and even then, they filter select data to protect their perceived insider`s edge. Working in a need-to-know environment can be frustrating and demoralizing to employees; such workers are handicapped in seeing the larger picture. Bosses who operate this way actually reveal their insecurities rather than any particular managerial strength.

Mimicry Behavior - Having a know-it-all demeanor . . .

Taking charge is one aspect of workplace supervision; but know-it-all managers who regularly dominate conversations and settings often appear to employees as condescending rather than authoritative. What is the source of know-it-all behavior? Some know-it-alls seem to be caught up in their own sense of importance and do not perceive others as worthy enough to have anything of merit to say. Maybe a few lack the ability to concentrate and listen. Still others appear to perceive talk as a way to control every situation. Whatever the source of this behavior, its negative consequences in the workplace are fairly apparent. Know-it-alls have an uncanny ability to turn off other people. Employees, for instance, eventually give up trying to conduct two-way conversations with them, and by not listening, these individuals not only lose valuable input from subordinates but from peers as well.

Mimicry Behavior - Losing sight of the truth . . .

Individuals constantly vying for the attention and approval of those in power seem to fall into the habit of saying whatever the boss wants to hear, and doing whatever it takes to get ahead. It appears to become a way of life for them. In time, they begin to deal with everyone in the same manner. And eventually, subordinates and peers learn they really cannot count on these colleagues for a straight answer - like the truth.

Mimicry Behavior - Short-term thinking . . .

The advent of executive compensation packages being tied to company performance seems to have encouraged a population of myopic thinkers in the executive suites of Corporate America. Within contemporary business environments, managers have tended to increasingly focus on quick ways to generate immediate profits (e.g., reducing headcount; scaling back benefits plans; hiring less expensive but less skilled talent; eliminating less profitable operations and product lines despite long-term potential). Executives do not appear to dwell on the notions of employee retention, worker morale, professional development, long-term business growth, etc. They apparently figure the next management team can worry about such details. Their focus seems steadfastly fixed on meeting short-term financial objectives, largely so they can realize the lucrative rewards and attractive perks provided through their employment contracts.

Mimicry Behavior - Being egocentric . . .

In textbooks, the manager`s role is often defined as a facilitator (i.e., the manager provides the tools and resources for the employee to get the job done). However, the boss who takes credit for the work of others and keeps employees far, far in the background appears to be much more common in the real world. Such egocentric managers tend to be somewhat cavalier about the well being of subordinates and often hold back stellar employees whose achievements they want to continue to usurp. How so? For example:


By unfairly tainting subordinates` work performances to deter other managers from offering the employees better positions.

By not releasing talented employees to accept transfers to or developmental assignments in other departments.

By misrepresenting to other managers competent employees` desires for new jobs.
One question related to an egocentric boss remains unanswered - how can you manage those around you when you are looking inward all of the time?

Mimicry Behavior - Forgetting your roots . . .

Have you ever come across a former colleague who has been promoted into a higher-level management position and now appears to be a stranger to you? It seems he forgot you shared a common professional bond a few years earlier. Why do executives forget where they started? Some may have become pretentious along the way and want to disassociate themselves from their more humble beginnings. Others may be insecure and prefer to distance themselves from those who knew their weaknesses in earlier days. Unfortunately, when executives forget from where they came, an inflated sense of self-importance tends to dominate their personalities and overshadow their interactions with others.

Mimicry Behavior - Being all style, little substance . . .

Given the dynamics of many contemporary business organizations, one cannot help but speculate that early on some insightful executive discovered a projection of success, despite contradicting realities, could take one as far, if not farther, than hard work. Here is a timeless example. When a senior-level executive schedules a visit to a business operation within his corporation, local management tends to spend thousands of dollars and countless employee hours enhancing the site for the V.I.P.`s brief stay. They have the facility painted, work areas are remodeled, new signage is posted and the factory is cleaned from top to bottom. The senior executive inherently knows these preparations were performed expressly for his arrival.

Yet, it does not phase him. He probably went through the same antics for some other visiting V.I.P. a few years earlier when he was climbing the corporate ladder. Throughout time, as the concept of style overshadowing substance has taken hold in the business world, it seems the art of individual self-promotion in work settings has proliferated as well. Despite the working world`s perceived appreciation of people who get the job done (i.e., "doers"), people who talk up ideas, rather than "doers," appear to get more recognition.

Typically, "doers" seem oblivious to the notion of self-promotion. They assume their completion of tasks sends a clear message to everyone in the organization. On the other hand, idea people tend to take time to make themselves visible in the workplace. Although style over substance appears to reign supreme in Corporate America, both idea people and their promotional flare as well as "doers" and their get-the-job-done focus are needed to round out a business entity.

ABOUT THE AUTHOR: Dr. Sarah Banda Purvis` credentials include two decades of managerial experience with two different Fortune 500 companies headquartered in the United States. When Dr. Purvis reflects upon her 20-year work experience in Corporate America, she describes it as an enlightening journey. Her corporate sojourn permitted her to examine workplace settings on a firsthand basis as well as to observe, listen to, and note the experiences of other working women. Interview Dos and Don`ts  
 Posted by  varma   love4vishal@rediffmail.com

BEFORE THE INTERVIEW


Try to learn all you can about the company and the interviewer.
There are many ways to find information about the company: the Internet, company brochures, financial statements, articles written about the company, its own employees. To find out about the interviewer may be a little tougher, but not impossible: subordinates or colleagues of the interviewer may provide valuable information, such as interviewing style, valued skills and qualities, special issues or problems being faced.


Match your strengths to the company`s expectations for the position you are seeking.
Extremely important. The better the match, the better your chances. If the position is advertised, some of the expectations may be clearly stated in the ad. Again, try to learn more. Then carefully match what you have to offer, to those expectations.


Prepare what questions you will ask.
At some point your interviewer will likely ask if you have any questions. Prepare in advance. Show your interest in the environment in which you will be working. Ask about the department`s organization, who you will report to, what autonomy employees have to be creative, to come up with new ways of doing things. Ask about what training will be available to enhance your performance capacity.


Prepare answers to questions you may be asked. Practice aloud.
One of the most neglected aspects: PREPARATION! If you are unprepared you may give answers you will regret later. List every question you think may be asked. Then write down how you will answer that question. Have someone review your answers, and discuss them. Practicing aloud will make you much more comfortable during the interview by providing greater self-confidence.


Arrive for the interview fifteen or twenty minutes early.
If you plan to arrive early, you may still be on time even if caught up in traffic. Those 15 or 20 minutes will give you the opportunity to go to the restroom just before the interview, to relax a bit, perhaps to briefly meet the receptionist or secretary and potentially find out more about the company or the interviewer.


Make a good impression. Be properly dressed and well groomed.
An initial bad impression is extremely difficult to overcome. Your interview attire should be somewhat above what you will generally wear on the job.


Do not take anyone with you.
This is your "show." Do not give the impression that you need "moral support" or that you may have child issues to deal with.

DURING THE INTERVIEW


Shake hands with your interviewer. Both men and women should have a firm, but not bone-crushing handshake.
A limp handshake projects lack of self-confidence. A firm handshake projects self-assurance.


Stand until invited to sit. Sit straight but relaxed.

Maintain good eye contact. Don`t stare or look down or continuously away from the interviewer.
Looking away too much gives the impression that you are not sure of yourself, that you are not a good communicator, that you may be hiding something.


Do not smoke or chew gum.

Listen carefully. Answer questions frankly and briefly. Speak with confidence but do not boast.
Being brief is very important. Remember that your interview time is limited. If you spend too much time talking, the interviewer may not be able to get a complete interview, to your disadvantage.


Show that your education, training, work experience and accomplishments fit you for the job you are seeking. Do not apologize for your lack of experience but show your flexibility.
You will be selected for a position because you are perceived to meet the requirements and expectations of the position, because you are perceived to fit in well in the organization, because it is perceived that you will do a good job. You are in effect selling yourself to the interviewer. If you lack some of the skills required, make the effort to acquaint yourself with the subject, be somewhat conversant with it, and sell your willingness to spend some of your own time acquiring the skills. Also stress your ability to learn quickly.


Don`t cry about hard luck, money difficulties, or personal problems.
If you "cry" during the interview, you are likely to cry after you are employed as well.


Do not criticize others - especially past employers or associates.

Do not ask about salary or benefits until you are made a firm job offer.
If you do, you will convey the impression that you are more interested in what you will get, than what you will give. The interview is designed to sell you for a position. The interviewer is trying to determine if you are a good fit. A discussion of salary or benefits before a hiring decision is made is counterproductive.


Ask for the job! Ask when it would be OK to call back to learn of the hiring decision.
If indeed you want the position (if not, don`t ask for it!). Let the interviewer know that you are very interested.

AFTER THE INTERVIEW


Thank the interviewer. Shake hands as you leave.

Obtain the mailing address of the interviewer. Write and mail a "Thank You" letter the same day.
Take this opportunity to reiterate the key points of fit for the position, and again indicate your interest in the position. This is good form, serves to remind the interviewer once again about you, and could impress simply because most people don`t do it.

Positively Speaking, Several years ago...  
 Posted by  varma   love4vishal@rediffmail.com

Several years ago I went to beautiful Rome, the ancient city of artistic splendor. I relished every moment spent along the narrow streets, savoring every turn and twist, inhaling every fragrance and being enthralled by each new sight. Like a child at Christmas, I excitedly anticipated what would come next.

It was all too much to absorb, a sensory overload of creative genius. None greater than the prolific work of Michaelangelo. Overwhelming is the best word to describe this contribution. To stroll the halls of the Vatican and pass through the grandeur of the Sistine Chapel, is to appreciate the imprint and influence of this great artist.

His works are many, his sculptures splendid. David, Day and Night, Madonna of Bruges, Twilight and Dawn, La Pieta, Medici Madonna and Child. My favorite statue of his is located in the Church of St. Peter and the Chains. The great Moses, a chiseled masterpiece of the man of God coming down from the Mount of Sinai, ready to present to the people of Israel the Law that would guide them.

To look at this work one must take time. Like examining a fine jewel, the light has to be just right and it must be seen from all angles. Truly, you see Moses and that is a veritable feast for the eyes.

As wondrous as it is, Michaelangelo wasn`t happy with the result of his efforts. When he had finished, he is reputed to have angrily cried out, "Why doesn`t thou not speak?" He wanted the great Moses to come to life. He felt he had failed and in his anger he took his chisel and struck the knee of the statue! The mark remains as a reminder of his dissatisfaction.

I understand this emotion-fueled attitude. People who are creative and desire to achieve are often dissatisfied with their efforts. They are often harsh and critical of themselves when perfection isn`t attained.

Winners in life don`t allow their perfectionist drives to so frustrate them that they quit. Michaelangelo didn`t. He continued working until he died. Never allowing his quest for perfection to destroy the art he could create. Believe me when I say that Michaelangelo was too hard on himself when it came to his Moses. It does indeed speak to you.

You may have a perfectionist streak that you`ve allowed to get in the way of accomplishment. "If it can`t be done perfectly, why try?" Don`t fall into that trap. Sometimes we`re just too close to our work to see the real worth of it. Michaelangelo was.

Enjoy the journey of effort and believe your work matters. Do your best and accept the results.

Resumes Rule You Out, Job Proposals Get You In!  
 Posted by  varma   love4vishal@rediffmail.com

If you think there have been a lot of changes in the job market in the last few years, fasten your seat-belts because the next few years will see things change even more dramatically.

This means that many of us are going to have to rethink our basic strategy about the ways in which our career will provide us a rewarding and successful work life. We have to consider new techniques and attitudes to help us find new jobs or secure new positions with current employers.

One such change is in the belief that resumes are still king when it comes to applying for a job. In the past, many job-seeking employees were under the misconception that a great resume was going to win them their dream job. Not necessarily true. Essentially, all resumes do is get you on the list of people to be considered. When a job posting goes out, companies are often deluged with resumes. It`s not uncommon that they see hundreds of resumes. Consequently, most hiring managers concentrate on ruling you out of consideration based strictly on your resume.

Though there are plenty of companies who still require a resume from prospective employees, resumes are rapidly becoming a thing of the past. And believe me, I`m happy to see them go. They caused me a bunch of anguish over the years. I could never figure out what my resume should look like. Should it be one or two pages, on white or colored paper? Start with my Career Objective statement or something else? List all my past jobs, including busboy, or just the "real" jobs that best positioned me for the future? Should it list references or not? You get the idea.

So what`s taking the place of the resume? What will help you get in front of the hiring manager or Human Resources department of your targeted next employer? What will clearly distinguish you from "the pack?" Take it from your Career Coach, a job proposal is the ticket. I can say that with authority because I personally have used a job proposal on four occasions and each time got the job I wanted (of course I did. I wouldn`t be telling you this story if I hadn`t).

Just what is a job proposal? It is a one or two page mini-business plan that you present to the decision-maker of the company you want to work with in order to get an interview. How you go about presenting the material is markedly different from a resume. A resume primarily tells what you have done in the past. A job proposal tells what you are going to do in the position you are applying for when you get it. A few examples of what you might include in the document:


A brief statement illustrating that you understand what the company`s vision is and what are some of the issues they face in achieving their vision. Companies` love it when you can talk intelligently about what they are up to, what some of their obstacles are, etc.

You might propose opening a new office for them in a city (yours) that they currently don`t have a presence in.

You can propose an entirely new position and why it will be of value to them.

You can propose coming to work for them as an independent contractor with the understanding that if they like you and your work, they can hire you full time (a sort of working interview).
The fact of the matter is that you can propose almost anything as long as you do it in a very business-like manner. The language you use should reflect both your professionalism and your passion for being part of their vision. But whether you are evaluated on the basis of a job proposal, a resume, or an interview, the goal is to differentiate yourself from the other candidates.

What We Have Here Is A Failure To Communicate-- With Clarity  
 Posted by  varma   love4vishal@rediffmail.com

Regardless of what each of us does for a living, we are all in the communication business. How clearly we express and understand ideas directly affects not only the opportunities that come our way, but also the level of personal satisfaction and happiness we achieve as human beings.

In a study which polled top executives in fifty-eight of America`s largest companies, every single executive named communication skills as a major factor in their advancement. Graduates of business schools continually report that communication skills are the most valued training they`ve received. And studies on divorce and human interactions prove time and again that poor communication is a major factor in failed relationships.

Though most of us are aware of these types of studies, we rarely place the importance on communication skills they deserve. Instead, we go through life wrestling with dozens of other seemingly unrelated issues - finances, personal relationships, child rearing conflicts, work relationships, career decisions and so on. As we struggle through the weeks and months grappling with these concerns, we never stop to consider that how well we verbally express our own ideas and understand the ideas others express to us affects each of these areas profoundly.

Why, if verbal communications skills are so important, do they remain such a low priority for most of us? For several reasons. One is simply the fact that we are all "too busy" in today`s rapid-fire world of business and person relationships to pay much attention to communication skills. We feel it`s all we can do to say what`s on our minds at the moment and hurry on to the next "fire fight." Perhaps the most prevalent reason, however, is simply the fact that we take verbal communications skills for granted. We make the mistake of assuming we are good verbal communicators with no room for improvement..

As Simple as Walking and Talking?

This misconception originates in childhood. We learn to walk and talk at a very young age, and we tend to subconsciously categorize the two skills in similar ways. In order to walk, we simply place one foot in front of the other and off we go. Once learned, the skill is repeated day after day and that`s that. Simple. And when we talk we simply repeat the process of saying what`s on our minds, right? One word after another, encounter after encounter, day after day. As simple as walking? Not by a long shot.

When closely examined, it turns out verbal communication is anything but a simple process. It`s more like a complex recipe - a rich, varied and at times even exotic blend of words, gestures, emotions, body language, opinions, preferences and many other influences that guide us through the continual stream of human interactions we take part in.

When the recipe is a perfect mix, we speak and we are understood; we listen and we understand. When the mix is wrong, we certainly continue speaking and listening, but a good deal of the "flavor" of our communication is lost in the exchange.

Those who become aware of this mix, and apply its principles as a model for their relationships, enjoy an extraordinary ability to get their ideas across, both on the job and in their personal lives. As you might guess, the benefits they reap are equally extraordinary. Verbal communication becomes a comfortable and rewarding process. Common frustrations are avoided. Stress is reduced. Time is saved. And interactions on the job and at home take on a new and exciting sense of human connection.

The Clarity Factor

One key ingredients in this verbal communication "recipe" is a subtle, but essential element that makes verbal communication so productive and rewarding when it`s at its best - brilliant clarity of word and thought, or what might be called The Clarity Factor. The Clarity factor is simply a measure of how clearly a person expresses thoughts when speaking, and how clearly that same person understands others, when listening. The higher the Clarity Factor, the better chances of success both in business and personal relationships.

And how does one achieve a high Clarity Factor? By putting four simple "Focal Points" to use as a communication style: 1. Focus, 2. Magnify, 3. Penetrate and 4. Listen.

1. FOCUS YOUR MESSAGE

In order to speak with clarity, we must first clarify our thoughts at the source - in our own minds. We can accomplish this with simple analysis and objectivity. Analysis simply means thinking over and deciding exactly what needs to be said. Most times is not what first comes to mind. Objectivity means removing personal emotions when inappropriate. This limits distortion.

Think of the focusing process as something like taking a photograph. If an image recorded on the negative is out of focus, there`s no hope of developing a clear picture from it. So it goes with our thoughts. If we aren`t clear in our own minds on what needs to be said, we can`t hope to communicate an idea clearly to someone else. By the same token, if we take a picture in perfect focus, but something causes distortion - for instance a fish-eye lens - this is similar to inappropriate emotional content. The image may be recorded in perfectly clear focus, but it is distorted, and thus still inaccurate. .

2. MAGNIFY THE LISTENER`S ATTENTION

Once we`ve clarified our thoughts, our next challenge is to become number one on the listener`s list of mental priorities. This may sound like a given, but stop to consider the amount and types of mental distractions those listening to us are continually dealing with - reports, family matters, budgets, e-mails, memos, telephone calls, meetings, target objectives, sales quotas. Of course, the list goes on. And what it adds up to is the fact that we must compete to be heard. We can use three simple ideas to win consistently at this competition.

If what we say is interesting, chances are good the person listening will give us their attention. If what we say is important, again, we have a good chance of gaining attention. But if we want to be sure to get the attention we require, there`s one simple, surefire way to accomplish it: Simply ask for the person`s undivided attention. Say something like, "I have something very important to tell you. Please stop everything else and listen to me." As you do this, look directly into the listener`s eyes. You should see mental connection - a sign that you can now speak.

There is one obvious criteria to keep in mind when using this method. The speaker must, indeed have something important to say or risk losing credibility with the listener in the future.

3. PENETRATE PERSONAL BARRIERS

Once we have the listener`s attention, our verbal "message" can be sent. The object at this point is to assure that it gets through as closely as possible to the way the we have visualized it. The listener`s opinions, values, and past experiences will act as personal barriers or "distortion factors" to this process.

For instance, if a manager says to an employee, "Our sales numbers are in big trouble all of a sudden." Much is left to interpretation. What exactly does "big trouble" mean for instance? How big? And how quick is "all of a sudden?" In this case, the employee will visualize his definition of "big trouble," and apply his time frame for "all of a sudden." This means the employee understands something different than what the manager has tried to communicate.

We have the ability to greatly disable these distortion factors, however, thus assuring that our words get through with maximum clarity. This is accomplished with the use of four techniques.

Visual Language - using visual and precise word choices avoids vague descriptions that can be easily misinterpreted. Use of examples and analogies provides a common frame of reference for the listener and the speaker. A request for feedback is appropriate when you`ve said something and the listener displays a quizzical look or seems distracted - as if they didn`t get the message. Finally, revision as needed simply means adjusting what you say when that quizzical or distracted look appears.

In our previous example for instance, instead of phrases like "big trouble" and "all of a sudden," the manager might have said, "Our sales completion figures took a twenty-eight percent nose dive in May. Two more months like this and we`ll have to let one person go." In this case, little is left to misinterpretation, and thus distortion. The employee imagines what the manager meant to say. This means his verbal communication has a very high Clarity Factor.

So, by using these three simple techniques one side of the verbal communication process is clarified greatly - speaking. But what about the other side - listening? Is it just as critical? Even more so.

4. LISTEN WITH CLARITY

Perhaps the most rewarding part of developing a high Clarity Factor comes when we gain the ability to reverse roles and become clarity receivers or listeners - in other words, when we are able to truly understand the other person`s message by letting down our own personal barriers. This process of understanding is accomplished when we`re able to: Empathize - place ourselves in the speaker`s position, and Visualize - Imagine what the person speaking is trying to communicate. In essence we take the speaker`s words at face value and learn to imagine how that person really feels as he or she speaks them. We ask ourselves what the words really mean to the speaker, and from that draw an interpretation.

What exactly happens when we do this? An unexpected, bi-product of verbal communication suddenly materializes. We gain an amazing, crystal clear view into a state that too often flashes by us as a momentary spark in the blur of our competitive, fast paced lives - the state of true human understanding.

By becoming clarity listeners, then, we begin to truly understand the world around us and the people who inhabit it. We begin t to hear "between the lines" and understand not just the words people say to us, but also the feelings behind those words. This leads to an increased sense of human sensitivity and truly effective verbal communication - in other words a top notch Clarity Factor. And the final result is a deep sense of personal empowerment and well-being that comes with it.

As an added benefit, listening with clarity has a compounding affect. It also allows us to accomplish the first three steps easier. Because we are better able to understand the people we talk with, we gain the ability to think out and even sense how to communicate with them on the clearest possible level.

Does this mean we should all spend your days cautiously analyzing every word we say? And maintaining the strictest concentration on every word we hear? Hardly. Little else would ever get done! What we should do is simply incorporate the four simple clarity "Focal Points" into our own communication style: Focus, Magnify, Penetrate and Listen. With just a little practice, they will become automatic and the benefits of using them will become-crystal clear.

A Microsoft Certified Systems Engineer rating might get your foot in the door, but experience is still what wins the job, say experts on our question.techies.com forum

Member:
I'm just finishing my MCSE certifications. All of the jobs call for a lot of experience, of which I have none. I'm not dumb. I have a bachelor's degree from Stanford, and a lot of business experience – I've owned several businesses (restaurants). How do I find jobs that are entry level?

Lawson:
It's been my personal experience that after earning a certification like the MCSE, the best way to get experience is to do some contracting through a staffing agency. I know that when I was earning my MCSE and was seeking a position, there wasn't much work available for new people. Many companies were getting their existing [employees] certified.

Having an MCSE and a degree from Stanford, you should be able to really utilize your business and technical expertise. I'd also take a look at some CIO positions, if you're open to something that doesn't require 100 percent hands-on skills. Most CIO positions are seeking someone who's experienced in the business world as well as in technology.

Member:
I am about to enter a two-year program, in which I'll obtain an A.A.S. degree, as well as the following certifications: A+, MCSE or CCNA, or both (it's possible, but rigorous.) Are these skills still in high demand, and which is preferable: the MCSE or the CCNA? Is there another certification that tops any of these? I'm fairly new to this and would like to know which direction is best before I start going down the wrong path.

Chapman:
My opinions are based on recent conversations I've had with recruiters and hiring managers. MCSEs with little or no prior IT work experience are becoming far too common in recruiter's inboxes lately. Hiring managers look at someone with no experience as a challenge whether they've proved they can pass some tests (MCSE) or not.

I'm not saying that the MCSE is not valuable. A recent article I read suggested that experienced IT professionals with their MCSE had 12 percent higher salaries than those with equal work experience but no certification. Unfortunately that doesn't do a lot of good when you try and compare 10 different applicants with no prior experience but all have their MCSE.

The CCNA hasn't been around as long. It hasn't received the same press as the MCSE, and Cisco has been a hot company recently. It's more of a supply and demand thing now (because the CCNA could easily go down the same path as the MCSE over the next few years). But the CCNA is a much better option in my opinion. Whatever you decide, getting some hands-on experience will provide the best outcome. Any certifications you pick up later will enhance that experience.

Krueger:
It sounds like you're already on your way to getting into the networking field by going for your MCSE. One thing to note is that, after passing one exam in the MCSE track, you are considered an MCP, a great certification for obtaining employment. So begin searching for employment immediately, don't wait until you complete all six exams for your MCSE.

Remember that your prospective employer is going to be concerned with experience. I can't count the number of people who rush off and obtain their MCSE and find out that their prospective employer still wants experience – even though they've obtained their MCSE. Its awesome that you've accelerated your technical knowledge to the degree that you have, but remember: Unless you can put this on your resume as a description of something you did as a part of your job, its difficult to say you have "experience."

That said, try and find some way in your current position as a retail manager to conduct some type of "techie project" at work. This is something you can then put on your resume as actual experience vs. actual knowledge.

Also, obtaining multi-platform certifications is very attractive to an employer. It's great that you're obtaining your MCSE, but most large companies run Novell as well, so having a CNE to go along with your MCSE is of tremendous advantage to them.

The Right Path: This Road ain't the Straight and Narrow

What will you be when you grow up….

Ever since I became old enough to talk, this is the question that has beleaguered me: what will you be when you grow up? My answers ranged from doctor and nurse to air hostess and teacher. Somehow, my becoming just an adult, without a profession or an aspiration for a job was not good enough. I had to be somebody, other than being just a name.

Have you gone crazy?

Today I am neither a doctor, nor teacher, nor an air hostess. I am a career consultant, the kind of profession that was unheard of when I was growing up. I wonder what my parents would have thought if I told them at age three or 15 that I was going to be a career consultant. Having never heard of this profession they would have thought I had gone crazy. Yet, it was something I never planned, but an option that gradually evolved in my life as being just the right choice.

Planning is the name of the game….

So it will be for many of you, who will end up working in professions that you or your parents have never heard about or you may work in traditional professions and move along a well planned path. But one thing is quite definite: only those of you who will work hard towards your studies, acquire new skills as you move along in life, involve yourself in cultural/sporting activities and attempt to plan your life will be successful.

Get what you want….

Why plan? In our educational system the competition is so stiff that not only will you require a plan but also an equally well developed alternative career plan or a fall-back plan. In fact, it is so much easier to jump right back into the race if you already have your next strategy well worked out. In the words of Henry C. Link "The truth about making a plan: it never works (not in the way you plan it, that is). If, however, you do make a plan, the chances of getting what you want significantly increase."

Avoid disappointments….

Let us take the case of Ranjeet who wanted to earn a lot of money and lead a glamorous life. He decided on hotel management as a career. Only, when he joined his first job at a very nominal salary and worked night shifts he realised that the bright lights and fast entertainment of the hotel had nothing to do with the employees. How do you think he could have avoided the disappointment? Or let us take the case of Suhail who had set his heart on joining the army, but got rejected in the medical examination because he was flat-footed. How do you think Suhail could have planned his life better? Ranjeet and Suhail, if they had researched their career choices in detail, might have saved themselves precious time and disappointment. Here's hoping that all of you will research the career of your choice well and not get taken in by superficial glamour and loose precious time because of false starts. All of us have limited energy and it is up to us to use it efficiently.

1001 Ways To Take Initiative At Work  
 Posted by  varma   love4vishal@rediffmail.com

by Kathy Simmons

"The biggest mistake you can make in life is to think you work for somebody else."
- Bob Nelson

Eighty-two percent of executives can`t be wrong. When polled with the question "What do you feel is the single best way for employees to earn a promotion and/or raise?" a whopping 82 percent responded with "Ask for more work and responsibility."

How well are you demonstrating initiative in your job? Sharpening this crucial character indicator will surely make a lasting impression on your boss, coworkers, and customers.

taken from

www.careermag.com




Bob Nelson`s 1001 Ways to Take Initiative At Work (Workman Publishing Company, 1999, $10.95), provides just what the doctor ordered for mastering the art of initiative. In keeping with management guru Nelson`s tradition of best-selling 1001 books, this latest 229-page addition is replete with thought-provoking quotes, real-life success stories, and tips. (Nelson is the author of 18 books and founder of Nelson Motivation, Inc., in San Diego, California.)

The book describes initiative as acting before circumstances force you to react, using foresight to avoid problems, and displaying the courage needed to take advantage of opportunities - especially those that are not obvious to others. Perhaps most importantly, it means trusting your instincts, and having the confidence to act upon them rather than waiting for someone else to do so.

Initiative matters because 1) managers seek, admire, and reward it, and 2) it is difficult to find. Most employees are aware of the value of initiative.

In fact, according to Nelson, a recent survey shows 55 percent of employees identified initiative as the best way to get ahead at work. That`s significantly higher than intelligence, for example, which generated a modest 12 percent vote.

As Nelson suggests in his book, when all is said and done, your career should be a result of your choices, not your boss` or coworkers` decisions. It`s easy to blame circumstances, fate or bad luck for a boring job or stalled career, but since when do high achievers take the easy road?

Nelson points out, "initiative is personal: the individual controls when, where, and how much initiative to take on the job. Even though its impact may be felt throughout an organization, initiative starts with the employee - what he or she can do on a daily basis."

Robert Townsend, former CEO of Avis, is credited by Nelson with offering the following advice "Make every decision as if you owned the whole company." Think about how this would change the energy level of an organization. What would happen if everyone felt responsible for the success of the organization? There would be no more "we-they" talk because all workers would realize everyone has the potential to have impact where they work today. A recent study described in 1001 Ways to Take Initiative At Work illustrates this well. When asked, senior management identified a mere four percent of the problems in an organization. When middle management, supervisors, and all staff were factored into the equation, the percentages increased at astounding rates: from nine percent to 74 percent to 100 percent respectively. Think about that the next time you are tempted to wait for upper management to recognize and fix an "obvious" problem!

1001 Ways to Take Initiative At Work is separated into three sections. "You and your job" focuses on creativity, doing your homework, making improvements, and maintaining persistence in your existing job responsibilities. "You and others" highlights the value of communication, working in teams, managing up, and creating influence in dealing with others. Finally, "Your career and your life" spotlights the value of learning, taking charge of your career, developing skills on the job, and overcoming obstacles to have your current position become a launching pad for your career.

Condensing the pearls of wisdom found in this book is a daunting challenge. This is not a collection of breakthroughs made by super-intelligent "gear heads" or high power executives. Rather, it consists of real-life examples of ordinary workers who made a difference in their job, and the consequences that resulted for the organization as well as themselves.

Examples of initiative range from simple "slam-dunks" to more complex undertakings:

"A woman working in accounts payable noticed many checks being sent to the same vendor every month. She took it upon herself to call the vendor, who confirmed it would be happy with just one check. The end result: fewer checks and less manpower." Does this example make you wonder how many potential opportunities are sitting right under your nose? They are there for the taking with just a little initiative.

An example with more far-reaching ramifications is that of McDonald`s, which struggled with its environmentally unpopular Styrofoam food containers. Did it take the president or a team of research scientists to recommend alternative packaging solutions? No. It was a mid-level employee who had the guts and confidence to do the research, and present his ideas, which, by the way, are still used worldwide today.

Employees with initiative are not content with complaining about circumstances or playing the victim role. A great example of the synergy initiative creates within a team is found in the story of one manager who heard employees complaining about problems with their laptops. She posted large sheets of paper in the cafeteria labeled "Problems" and "Solutions," and encouraged everyone to post and answer questions. The communication and good will this act of initiative accomplished were immeasurable!

Another heartwarming example of initiative involves a customer service technician at a cable television company. When he received a service call from a customer who was confused about which channel was where on the cable system, the technician realized that his customer couldn`t read the channel lineup card because cataracts obscured her vision. He quickly returned to the office, enlarged the card on the copy machine, laminated it, and then personally delivered it to his excited and grateful customer. That`s the epitome of initiative!

As an employee, you will find inspiration to take risks, and pounce on opportunities in your workplace as you read examples of people just like you who made a difference. If you manage others, purchasing a copy of this book for all your staff would be a wise investment. Make it "required reading." You`ll find it a potent catalyst for inspiring and motivating a group of employees who may not be tapping into their real potential.

Want to put your career on a faster track? Take the initiative now. Go to your local bookstore, get a copy of 1001 Ways to Take Initiative At Work, curl up with a cup of tea, and enjoy the reading. You will be guaranteed to find inspiration and a feeling of "hey, if they can do it, why can`t I?" What a great first step towards sparking the initiative that can help your career skyrocket.
